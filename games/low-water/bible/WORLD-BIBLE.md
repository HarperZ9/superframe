# Low Water: story and world bible

Draft 1, 2026-10-16; continuity corrections 2026-10-17 (timeline order, 22:16, the conversion and the lighting a winter apart). Private. The game's content licence is the author's decision D5, still open,
so nothing here is published. "Low Water" is a working title. Every name, line and place in this
document is original. Disco Elysium and Zero Parades are the author's reference for ambition and
tone only; no names, lines, systems as designed or art come from them or any other game.

Design prose is plain technical English. Text that would appear in the game is set in quotation
blocks and uses the house voice described in section 2.

## 1. Premise

A loss assessor arrives in Gullet, a tidal harbour town, on the night its great lamp went dark. The
assessor works for the Mutual Indemnity of the Lower Shelf, an insurer that may not exist any more.
The claim that brought the assessor here is impossible on its face: the Harbour Board reported the
lamp maliciously darkened three hours before it went dark. By dawn the assessor must write a verdict
in the Daybook: whose fault the dark is.

Nobody fired a gun. The witnesses are the tide, the fog, the rain, the wind and the light, and the
assessor reads them with five instruments that answer back. The truth is not one culprit. It is a
chain of small, ordinary decisions, each defensible on its own, that together put a town's light
out and nearly drowned forty people. The thriller is in who knew, when, and who will pay for saying
so.

> CLAIM 4471/G. Harbour Board of Gullet against the Mutual Indemnity of the Lower Shelf.
> Nature of loss: darkness, malicious. Time of loss: see below. Time of claim: three hours before
> the time of loss.
>
> The assessor read that line twice on the ferry, and once more on the gangway, in case it would be
> ashamed of itself by the third reading. It was not.

## 2. Tone and voice

**What the game feels like.** Tense, funny, sad and strange, in that order of frequency. The town
is grotesque and the game is fond of it. Fear is social and atmospheric: a door that was open at
dusk is shut at midnight, and someone shut it. There are no jump scares, no monsters and no gore.
The one thing that is never explained (section 12) stays quiet and small.

**The house voice.** The narration is the assessor's report being written as the night happens. It
is itemised, dry and exact, and the feeling leaks through the precision. It refers to the assessor
in the third person, as a claims report would. It never tells the player what to feel. It notices.

Rules for the house voice:
1. Concrete before abstract. A wet step, a smell of paraffin, a number on a gauge. Then, if at all,
   what it means.
2. Jokes come from exactness, not from winking. The narration is never sarcastic about the people.
3. One grotesque image per beat, not three.
4. Short and long sentences alternate without a pattern. No sentence is built to land.
5. Characters speak in their own registers (section 7). The narration does not imitate them.
6. Numbers are real numbers from the environment state, rendered as a person would say them
   ("a little after eleven", "the glass at 996 and falling"), and they are always true.

> Item: one harbourmaster, wet. The assessor declines to estimate how wet. The estimate would be
> challenged.

> The quay smells of tar, of brine, and of a fish that has been dead long enough to have opinions.

## 3. Gullet

### 3.1 Geography

Gullet sits at the back of a long, narrow bay that the sea empties twice a day. At high water the
harbour is a basin of black water under the quay. At low water it is a plain of grey mud, pools and
ribs of old wrecks, and the town can walk out on it. The bay narrows at its mouth to a shoal called
the Spit. Ships enter by a dog-legged channel that is safe only when the great lamp marks it.

The town climbs from the harbour up Chancel Hill in terraces of narrow, leaning houses. Four
districts matter to the game (section 4). A ferry, the *Saint Cuthwin*, crosses the bay to the
railhead at Hesk twice a night. There is no road worth the name.

### 3.2 The tide

The tide is the town's clock and its law. Everyone in Gullet knows the hour of low water the way
other towns know the church bell. The night of the game is the lowest spring tide in nineteen
years. The town calls it Low Night and treats it as a holiday: families walk the harbour floor with
lanterns and pick over what the sea has given back.

> The sea does not give things back. It sets them down where you can see them, and waits to see
> what you do.

### 3.3 Light

Gullet's light has three ages, and every district still shows one of them.
- **Paraffin** (the old town, the Gut, the Brine): warm, smoky, flickering. Colours read true.
- **Sodium** (Lamp Quay, since the year the sodium came, two years ago): low-pressure sodium street
  lamps, a single orange line of the spectrum. Under them every colour is a shade of the same
  orange-grey. People in Gullet say a quarrel under the sodium cannot be won, because nobody can
  tell whose face went red.
- **White lamps on the hill** (Chancel Hill): cold, bluish street lamps the Municipal put up first,
  as a trial, and never took down.

Mother, the great lamp, stands over all three ages on the end of the mole.

## 4. Districts

Each district has its own light regime, its own way the environment enters play, and a look built
from engine features (see the look bible and the systems document for the mapping).

### 4.1 Lamp Quay (the vertical slice)

The harbour front: a stone quay with steps down to the floor, the Brine (a public house built in an
upturned hull), the weigh-house with the town balance on its roof, the harbour office, and the mole
running out to Mother. A row of crooked houses leans over it, five storeys of plaster in oxblood,
mustard, teal and bone that nobody has seen in colour after dark for two years.

- Light: three sodium lamps on swan-neck posts; the Brine's doorway and portholes in paraffin;
  lanterns on the floor on Low Night.
- Environment in play: the tide opens the steps and the floor; spray off the mole wets anyone who
  walks it in a westerly; the sodium erases colour evidence; a blackout drops the quay into
  dither-quantised light.

> Lamp Quay at low water. The steps go down further than steps should. At the bottom the town is
> walking on the floor of the sea with lanterns, very politely, like guests in a house whose owner
> has stepped out.

### 4.2 The Gut

The fish-market lanes behind the quay, between smokehouses with bent chimneys. Brine vats and an
open gutter run through the lanes; at night the gutter glows faintly, a slick of oil, scales and
something the Guild will not discuss, and it moves in slow bands of violet. The Worshipful Guild of
the Gut keeps its hall here.

- Light: paraffin lamps on wires and the fire-doors of the smokehouses. Rendered with
  palette-locked lighting: the district has a fixed palette, and the gutter and vats cycle through
  it.
- Environment in play: smell and smoke carry downwind, so the wind decides who knows what; a
  smokehouse's soot on a wall dates when it was fired; cold drives the stallholders indoors.

> The Gut is a lane the width of an argument. Eels hang over it in strings, and the gutter in its
> middle runs a colour that fish should not be.

### 4.3 Chancel Hill

The terraces above the town: the Alderman's house, the Municipal substation and, at the top, the
disused gasholder frame that the Congregation of the Filament has made into a church. The Old Bulb,
Mother's own lamp before she was electrified anew, hangs inside it over an altar ringed with
candles. The faithful kneel with glass bell jars over their heads.

- Light: candles; the white trial lamps along the path; the town's sodium glow below.
- Environment in play: fog lies in the harbour and leaves the hill clear, so from the hill you can
  see what the quay cannot; wind on the frame makes it sing; frost at dawn holds footprints that
  rain would have taken.

> The Congregation does not say the Old Bulb broke. It says the Old Bulb was opened.

### 4.4 The Flats and the drowned lamp

The harbour floor and the bay beyond, open only near low water. A hundred and forty years ago the
town's first lighthouse, the Old Light, stood on a rock that the bay later swallowed. Its broken
base shows at the lowest tides, leaning, a hole in its side. The town says that when Mother sleeps,
the Old Light wakes.

- Light: none, except what people carry, the ferry's lamp and Mother's beam when she burns. Fog off
  the bay lifts the distance to paper white. Rendered in four-tone dither lighting.
- Environment in play: the tide is the gate and the clock; fog hides and overhears; rain and the
  flood erase prints; Mother's beam, once relit, reveals in sweeps.

> The fog took the town at twenty past eleven. It did not take it all at once. It took the far
> things first, like a creditor.

## 6. The assessor

**Assessor H. Lamb.** Night-shift loss assessor of the Lower Shelf, eleven years in post, sent by
the evening ferry. The first name is never given. Everyone in Gullet guesses it (Hector, Hilda,
Hope, Horace, Hepzibah), and each guess says more about the guesser than about Lamb. Lamb carries a
claims case, the Kit (section 9) and the Daybook, a notebook with a cracked spine. Lamb was on the deck of the *Saint Cuthwin* when
it nearly struck the Spit, and saw the low light in the drowned lamp that turned it. Lamb is a
witness from the first minute, and the game uses that: Lamb's own memory is a clue like
any other, and can be wrong in one detail (the time, which Lamb took from a ship's clock that runs
four minutes fast).

The player decides what Lamb says. The narration decides what Lamb notices. Lamb has no arc of
redemption to complete. Lamb has a verdict to write, and has to live in the town it makes.

> The assessor's coat had been dry on the ferry. It has been dry for eleven years, as a matter of
> professional pride. It is not dry now.

## 7. Principal characters

Eight principal characters. Six speak in the vertical slice (Wenna, Keel, Marrow, Fennick, Sabine,
Merrow); Crake speaks by telephone in the slice and in person in the full game. Each entry gives
the role, the want, the fear, the secret, where the character was on the night, how they look, how
they speak and three lines in their voice.

### 7.1 Wenna Tallow, keeper of Mother

Sixty-four. Keeper for thirty-one years, the last six unpaid. Paraffin burns on both hands from the
old lamp. Knees that have stopped forgiving the hundred and twelve stairs.

- Want: to keep Mother, and to be let alone with her.
- Fear: that Mother will be found out, and that she will be the one who did it.
- Secret: for six years she has sold Mother's glass prisms to Deacon Fennick as relics and put
  green bottle glass in their place, cast in the Brine's bread oven from the Brine's own empties.
  For three years she has not climbed the stairs at night. Mother comes on with the quay lamps
  since the rewiring, and Wenna has sat in the Brine.
- On the night: in the Brine from 19:30, by the stove. She heard Mother's motor stop at 21:40 and
  did not go.
- Look: small, round-backed, a head too large for the oilskin, white hair cropped with a knife.
  The oxblood keeper's coat she was never paid to wear.
- Voice: short sentences. Calls Mother "she" and "her" and the assessor "you". Answers questions
  about herself with facts about the lamp.

> "She's not dark. She's got her eye shut. There's a difference, and you'll learn it or you'll
> go home on the morning boat."

> "A hundred and twelve stairs. I counted them going up for twenty-eight years. I count them now
> from the bottom, looking."

> "Glass is glass. She doesn't know the difference. Ships might. Ships are fussy."

### 7.2 Absalom Keel, harbourmaster

Fifty-eight. Harbourmaster for nineteen years. Eleven years ago, on the night Mother was converted
to electric light, he fell from the mole during the ceremony. He has been wet ever since. Nobody
can explain it and he does not try. Water runs from his cuffs. He leaves a puddle where he stands.
Chairs in Gullet have a drain hole for him.

- Want: a new lens, paid for by the Shelf, and his signature never read aloud.
- Fear: that the works order he signed will be found.
- Secret: he signed the rewiring that put Mother on the quay board. He knew the Guild's vote on Low
  Night would pull the quay switch. He filed the claim at 18:30 for "anticipated malicious
  darkening by wreckers", cited Merrow's threats, and let the dark happen. He has seen the lens
  turn green and suspects why.
- On the night: in the harbour office from 18:00; on the quay from 21:45, giving orders nobody
  followed.
- Look: very tall, narrow, a navy greatcoat black with water, a hat that funnels. His face is the
  grey of a thumb kept in a bath.
- Voice: the Board's voice. Speaks of himself as "the Board". Long sentences with official words.
  Drips at the commas.

> "The Board has been wet for eleven years, assessor, and has never once been damp in its
> convictions."

> "Wreckers. The word is old and so is the crime. A light goes out, a ship goes in, and in the
> morning there is copper on the mud and nobody's footprints."

> "Do not stand there. That is where the Board stands. You will find it is already wet."

### 7.3 Ottilie Crake, Alderman

Sixty. Chair of the Gullet Lamp Trust and of the Municipal Light and Coke Company, which share an
address, a ledger and her. She brought the sodium to the quay and the white trial lamps to the
hill, and she means every word she says about safety.

- Want: the automation judged a success; the Trust solvent; her name on the new light.
- Fear: colour. She has a theory that it distracts people.
- Secret: she struck the clause in the engineer's specification that required a keeper on duty to
  restrike Mother's arc after any loss of power, and she stopped Wenna's wages "pending a review"
  that never sat.
- On the night: at home on Chancel Hill; by telephone to the harbour office from 22:00.
- Look: upright, precise, dressed in grey that reads the same under every light, by intention.
  Spectacles with yellow lenses for night.
- Voice: modern, numerical, warm in a way that tells you she has rehearsed warmth.

> "Colour is a daytime extravagance. At night a town needs to see, not to admire."

> "Lumens per shilling, assessor. You may put that on my headstone, and you may light it with
> sodium."

> "Nobody was dismissed. A post was reviewed. The review is ongoing. I am sure it will be
> favourable."

### 7.4 Marrow Dunlin, shop steward of the Guild of the Gut

Forty-five. Fishmonger, orator, the largest man on the quay and the gentlest with a knife. He runs
the Guild's annual vote on the year's price, held on Low Night, by smell, blindfolded, in the dark,
as the Guild has done for thirty years. "A fish is honest in the dark. So is a man who has to
smell it."

- Want: the strike, won; the vote, kept sacred.
- Fear: that the vote will be blamed and the tradition ended.
- Secret: at 21:38 he pulled the quay switch in the weigh-house, as his father did. He did not know
  Mother was on it. When she stayed dark after 21:52 he guessed, and he has told no one.
- On the night: in the weigh-house from 21:00. After the vote the Guild stays to drink to the strike (the after-vote) until past midnight.
- Look: vast, aproned, his forearms shining with scales that never wash off. A blindfold still
  round his neck.
- Voice: a preacher of fish. Describes everyone as a fish. Loud, and then very quiet for the true
  parts.

> "You smell of train and paper. A flatfish. Both eyes on one side of your head, lying on the
> bottom, looking up at everyone."

> "We vote in the dark because in the light a man votes with his face."

> "Thirty years that switch has gone down and come up. Thirty years nothing went with it."

### 7.5 Ambrose Fennick, deacon of the Congregation of the Filament

Fifty. Twelve years ago he was the electrician's mate who carried Mother's old lamp down the stairs
when she was converted. On the way down the filament broke, and he says the light came out of it
and into him. He founded the Congregation within the year. It has forty members and a gasholder.

- Want: more splinters of the Eye (Mother's prisms) for the reliquary; the Old Bulb honoured.
- Fear: that the splinters will be called stolen, and the faith called theft.
- Secret: he buys the prisms from Wenna for coin and silence, and he knows the replacements are
  bottle glass, because he has held both up to a candle.
- On the night: leading a blessing of the harbour floor on Low Night, on the steps, from 22:30.
- Look: tall, narrow-shouldered, a white surplice over oilskins; a bell jar carried under his arm
  like a hat.
- Voice: soft, ecstatic, technical. Mixes the language of wiring with the language of prayer.

> "We do not say it broke. We say it was opened."

> "Every prism of the Eye is numbered. The Lord is an engineer, assessor. He leaves a schedule."

> "Low light, low water, low voices. Speak to me in the dark, and I will tell you something true."

### 7.6 Sabine Brine, landlady of the Brine

Fifty-two. Keeps the Brine, sells pickles, lends money, buries arguments. Everyone owes her a
little and she prefers it that way. She has known Wenna since they were girls on the floor at low
water.

- Want: Wenna safe; the Brine neutral; the tab paid.
- Fear: the Lamplighters, who have started drinking at the Brine and listening.
- Secret: she casts Wenna's green prisms in the bread oven from empty bottles, on the nights the
  oven is lit for bread. She fired it tonight at 20:00.
- On the night: behind the bar, all night.
- Look: wide, quick, sleeves rolled, a pickling fork behind one ear like a pencil.
- Voice: prices. Every sentence has a cost in it. Lies with total confidence and a smile that
  invites you to admire the lie.

> "Pickled egg, a penny. A straight answer, tuppence. I'm out of straight answers. The egg's very
> good."

> "Wenna left at nine to light up. I remember because the clock struck and she didn't."

> "Bottles? I sell what's in them. What's left is between me and the oven."

### 7.7 Tobias "Tub" Merrow, master of the Low Company

Forty. Salvor. Works the harbour floor at low water for copper, lead and anything the town drops.
The Board has wanted him gone for years, and tonight it has a word for him.

- Want: to be left alone, and the copper from the drowned lamp's old lantern gear.
- Fear: the rope. Gullet has not hanged anyone in sixty years, and Keel talks about it as if it
  were a tide.
- Secret: from 22:16 he was inside the drowned lamp's base stripping copper, a lantern hung in the
  hole. He believes his lantern is the low light that turned the *Saint Cuthwin* off the Spit at 22:20. He
  saved forty people while committing a theft, and he cannot say the one without the other.
- On the night: the floor from 22:06, the drowned lamp from 22:16 to 23:40, the Brine after.
- Look: short, wide, wet to the thighs from the gully, a coil of rope on one shoulder, a cold pipe.
- Voice: laconic. Measures time in tides. Never asks a question.

> "Nobody honest sleeps at low water. That's when the sea shows you what it took."

> "I didn't see a ship. I saw a light on the water, going the wrong way. Then it went the right
> way."

> "Copper doesn't care who owns it. Copper's a better citizen than most."

### 7.8 Pip Gannet, pot girl at the Brine (supporting)

Nineteen. Reads the tide by the water line on her own knees when she wades. Runs messages, pulls
pints, minds the pneumatic tube terminus in the weigh-house for a shilling a week. She is the
first person to see the Shelf's messages arrive, and in the full game the question of who types
them comes to her door (section 12).

> "Tide's on the turn. I can feel it in the backs of my legs, like a dog wanting out."

## 8. The Lamplighters

Crake's Municipal staff: six men and women with long brass poles and dark coats who service the
lamps and, since the sodium, watch the quay. They are the game's pressure, not its villains. Under
the sodium they can see the assessor and report who the assessor talks to. In a blackout they
cannot. They never threaten; they write things down, and the next person the assessor speaks to
already knows. Their sergeant, **Ida Gorm**, speaks only in questions.

> "And who might you be, at this hour? And would you like to tell me, or shall I wait?"

## 9. The Kit: instruments that answer back

Five instruments in a leather case, Shelf issue, each older than the assessor's post. Each reads
part of the environment state and says what it reads, in a temperament of its own. They are the
game's checks (systems document, section 3). The voices are short; the readings are exact.

| Instrument | Reads | Temperament | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Candlemas**, a brass photometer | Light level, direct-to-ambient balance, the kind of light (sodium, paraffin, white, daylight) and the colour of a thing in it | Vain, exact, refuses to name a colour it cannot see | Will not name hue under sodium; needs a light source to read colour |
| **The Glass**, a pocket barometer | Pressure and its trend, so the weather an hour ahead; and rumour pressure, how much the town knows of what Lamb has said | A gossip; enjoys bad news | Forecasts an hour, not two |
| **The Almanac**, a tide table with a brass dial | Tide height now and for the next six hours; when each step, gully and floor opens and closes | Precise, with a grudge against the moon | Predicts the sea, not the people on it |
| **The Damp**, a hair hygrometer | Humidity; wetness of a surface, a coat, a person; how long ago something was wet, from how far it has dried | Melancholy; the hair is from Assessor Pollard, retired or deceased (the paperwork disagrees) | Saturates in rain; must be dried at a fire to read again |
| **The Vane**, a tin cockerel on a folding stick | Wind direction and speed; where sound, smell, smoke and spray are carried | Excitable, points with its whole body | Reads where it is held, not elsewhere |

> CANDLEMAS: "I will not be party to that orange. Ask me again in honest light."

> THE GLASS: "Nine hundred and ninety-six and falling, darling. Someone is about to say something
> they can't take back."

> THE ALMANAC: "Low water at two minutes to eleven. The moon is, as ever, responsible, and, as
> ever, absent."

> THE DAMP: "Wet. Everything here is wet. I was a man's hair once. He was never dry either."

> THE VANE: "West! West! Everything said on this quay tonight goes east. Mind your mouth on the
> windward side."

## 11. The central mystery

### 11.1 The question

The claim says Mother was darkened maliciously by wreckers. The Shelf asks the assessor to find
the cause of the loss and to apportion fault. The game's question is "whose fault is the dark?",
and the honest answer is shared.

### 11.2 The truth, in layers

The player can reach each layer in order; each needs the one before it to be read correctly.

1. **The mechanism.** At 21:38 the Guild pulled the quay switch for its vote. Mother was on that
   board. She went out with it at 21:40.
2. **Why she was on it.** Seven months ago the Trust rewired Mother onto the quay board to save the
   rent of her own feeder and to automate her. Crake struck the clause that required a keeper to
   restrike the arc after any power loss. Keel signed. Nobody told the Guild.
3. **Why she stayed dark.** When power returned at 21:52 the arc was too hot to restrike by itself.
   It needed a hand at the panel. The keeper had not been paid for six years and had not been in
   the lamp room at night for three. She heard Mother stop and did not go.
4. **Why the claim came first.** Keel knew the Guild's vote would pull the switch on Low Night. He
   filed the claim at 18:30 and named the salvors, so that the Shelf would pay for a new lens and
   nobody would read his signature.
5. **Why he wanted a new lens.** A third of Mother's prisms are green bottle glass. Wenna sold the
   originals to Fennick over six years; Sabine cast the copies. Mother's beam had fallen to about a
   third of its rated strength and turned faintly green. The town never noticed, because the
   sodium hid the quay from her light. Ships noticed. A full lens claim would replace the evidence
   at the Shelf's expense.
6. **Who saved the ship.** The low light in the drowned lamp was, by every account but the clocks,
   Merrow's lantern, hung there while he stole copper from Trust property. The man the claim names
   saved the ferry, or was standing where the ferry was saved (section 12).

### 11.5 The verdict

The Shelf asks for a cause. Lamb can name one party, or split the blame between several, but can
only blame someone Lamb can make a case against with what is in the Daybook. The slice asks for a
provisional cause at midnight. The full game takes the verdict at dawn. The main verdicts and what
they cost:

| Verdict | What Lamb needs | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Malice by wreckers (the claim as filed) | Nothing beyond the claim | The Shelf pays for a new lens; the evidence of the green glass is replaced; Merrow is charged; the man who saved the ferry is punished for it |
| Negligence of the Guild | C2, C15 | The strike is broken by a fine; the vote is banned; the Trust and Board are untouched |
| Negligence of the Trust and Board | C3, C4, and C14 for Keel | The policy is void for the insured's negligence; the town gets nothing; Crake falls; Keel is exposed; the Guild is spared |
| Fraud by the keeper | C8 or C9, C6 or C5 | The lens was never worth its insured value; the claim is void; Wenna is ruined; Fennick's relics are seized; Sabine is ruined with her |
| Act of the sea | C13 and the tide | A small payment; nobody punished; the truth filed nowhere |
| Split blame (full game) | A case against each party named | The town's next night is set by who was blamed: each party's fall or survival changes the districts, the lights and who will talk to Lamb |

No verdict is the right one. The town lives with whatever Lamb writes, and the epilogue shows it
through its light: which lamps burn the next night, and in which colour.

## 12. Threads beyond the slice

- **Does the Shelf exist?** Its messages arrive by pneumatic tube in the weigh-house, typed on a
  machine with a sticking "e". Its chits are honoured less as the night goes on, as the rumour
  that it has failed spreads. Pip Gannet minds the tube. The full game lets the player find out
  who has been typing, and the answer is not a twist; it is a person with a reason.
- **The light in the drowned lamp.** Everyone, Merrow included, assumes his lantern turned the
  ferry. Merrow says his lantern went out when he knocked it climbing into the hole at 22:18, and
  he relit it at 22:25. The ferry turned at 22:20 by the true clock (22:24 by the ship's clock,
  which Lamb read). For those five minutes nothing of Merrow's was burning. The game leaves this
  one measurement in the gap and never closes it. Every instrument agrees on the time; nobody
  agrees on what was burning.
- **Keel's water.** Never explained. The Damp reads him at full saturation at all times, which makes
  him the only person in Gullet the instrument cannot judge, and the only one who knows it.
- **The Old Bulb.** If Mother is relit with a whole lens, the Congregation loses its relics. If the
  Old Bulb is restored to her, the faith has a god in a working lamp. The full game's final night
  can turn on it.

## 13. Glossary

- **Low Night**: the holiday of the year's lowest tide.
- **Mother**: the great lamp on the mole; officially the Gullet Head Light.
- **The Old Light**: the drowned lighthouse on Hound Rock.
- **The Shelf**: the Mutual Indemnity of the Lower Shelf, the assessor's employer.
- **The Kit**: the assessor's five instruments.
- **The Daybook**: the assessor's notebook: what Lamb has noticed, who said what, and the verdict.
- **The quay board**: the switchboard in the weigh-house that feeds Lamp Quay, and now Mother.
- **Restrike**: relighting an arc lamp after a power loss; Mother's needs a hand once she is hot.
- **Splinters of the Eye**: the Congregation's name for Mother's prisms.
- **The Lamplighters**: the Municipal's lamp crews, who also watch.
- **Chit**: a Shelf expense voucher; the town's acceptance of it falls with the Shelf's reputation.

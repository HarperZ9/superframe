# Literary bible 1: devices, allegory to diction

Part of [../LITERARY.md](../LITERARY.md). Story lead, 2026-10-22. Each device has three parts:
what the content already does (quoted, `file:line`, paths from the project root), the gaps, and
proposals tied to the mechanics. Every sample line is a **PROPOSAL** for the author to approve; no
existing scene is edited by this bible.

Abbreviations: `q_ned` is `content/quota/q_ned.lwd`, and so on for every `q_*.lwd` file. `HOUSEHOLDS`,
`STORY`, `SYSTEMS`, `MEDIA` and `WORLD` are `design/quota/02-HOUSEHOLDS.md`, `03-STORY.md`,
`04-SYSTEMS.md`, `08-WORLD-MEDIA.md` and `01-WORLD.md`.

## 1. Allegory

**What exists.** The Quota is built as an allegory of famine by false report, with its mechanism taken
from named research cases and never named in the game (PITCHES.md section 5). The slice carries the
whole chain in one day: an inflated return (`q_ned:14`, "HALDANE BARNS. YIELD 310"), the quota raised
on it (`q_office:57`, `shift(owed, 120, haldane_yard)`), export during hunger (`q_start:18`, the sacks
"stencilled HALVARD GRAIN CO."), the blame put on a people (`q_office:35`), and the bowl gone from the
table a week later (`q_coda:19`). Pask states the official reason in plain words: "Every sack this plain
sends up keeps a factory in Arlen open, and every factory buys a tractor from Halvard in the spring"
(`q_office:14`). The allegory works because nobody in the room speaks for an idea; each person speaks for
a sack, a post or a child.

**Gaps.** The allegory audit is in [06-ALLEGORY-AUDIT.md](06-ALLEGORY-AUDIT.md). In brief: era 2 maps
closely onto one historical case (quotas, blacklisting and passes all at once), and the Directorate's
naming leans on it. A one-to-one map turns allegory into costume, and a player who recognises the
costume stops asking the question about themselves.

**Proposals.**
- Keep each era's instrument a hybrid of at least two research anchors, so no era is a costume of one
  regime. Era 2 already folds in the price mechanism (QUOTA-DESIGN decision 1); make it visible in the
  slice's wide lens by a price board that rises between the dawn view and the coda (R3 and R4 exist in
  `q_community:152-167`; the board is the missing prop).
- Give the allegory a second reading that implicates the player's own world: the requisition logic is
  the logic of every target that is reported upward (a sales figure, a crime statistic, a test score).
  This is carried by one prop per era that uses a modern form for an old lie: in era 5, a council
  performance dashboard on the archive wall shows the Upper Town's "clearance target met".

## 2. Alliteration

**What exists.** Little, and correctly so in narration. The slice's sound patterning is mostly sibilance
in the dawn view: "Soldiers stand along it with their rifles slung while men load sacks stencilled"
(`q_start:18`), where the hiss of the s sounds carries the cold and the sacks. Holt's "Mud-hands. My
mother was one." (`q_granary:21`) puts the slur and the mother on one consonant, which binds them.

**Gaps.** Alliteration belongs where people in the world use it to persuade. The in-world notices have
none: `cl.hesh_hoard` reads "THE HESH HAVE THE GRAIN UNDER THE GOATS" (`q_discourse:22`), which has the
h-h-h pattern by accident and could carry it on purpose.

**Proposals.** Use alliteration only inside the carriers of rhetoric, so the sound itself becomes a sign
of manufacture that the examination can name (SMALL PRINT, "What is it made of?", DISCOURSE-SYSTEM
section 4). Never in narration or in the read-back.
- PROPOSAL, era 2 notice: "HOLD NO HOARD. HESH HOLES ARE HUNGER'S HOME." SMALL PRINT on examine:
  "Somebody in Arlen sat down and made all those words start the same."
- PROPOSAL, era 4 poster (Paul Ashby): "Edrin's Bread for Edrin's Bairns."
- PROPOSAL, era 5 feed slogan: "Clear the Clip Kids Out."
- A children's skipping rhyme in era 3 that has kept the era 2 notice's sound after its meaning has
  gone; the player hears it in the Mott house yard. This is the propaganda surviving as play.

## 3. Allusion

**What exists.** Allusion is internal. The altarpiece is "the Sower", whose field is the plain
(`MEDIA:63`); the faith is the Furrow Church, "the field as the gift, the harvest blessed, sin as
waste" (`WORLD:43`). The phrase "Received and found correct" (`q_ned:70`) is a bureaucratic formula
the dinner quotes back (`q_dinner:108`). The grain "running out round him like sand in a glass"
(`q_granary:103`) alludes to the hourglass and to time running out for the boy.

**Gaps.** Two allusions sit unused. The granary that stores grain for export during a famine is the
reverse of the oldest famine story in the scriptural tradition, where a granary is filled in the fat
years to feed people in the lean ones. And the Furrowe family shares its name with the Furrow Church,
which the content never touches.

**Proposals.**
- Write the Furrow Church's scripture as original text whose structure echoes the store-against-famine
  story without quoting it. The minister reads it on day 41 to a church with the altarpiece covered
  (`MEDIA:63`). PROPOSAL, a reading: "And the steward said, fill the long barn while the corn stands,
  for the field gives and then it rests." The granary on Exchange Square is the long barn, and it is
  being emptied onto a train.
- The Furrowe name: Abel says the family took it from the church's first field. PROPOSAL, Abel: "My
  grandad used to say we were named for the first furrow. He never said who ploughed it." Hold the
  question; never answer whether the name was given, bought or earned (it mirrors the census naming of
  Ilo Haldane, `HOUSEHOLDS:75`).
- Allusion across eras inside the game is the stronger tool: the era 5 memorial's "the hard winter"
  (`MEDIA:79`) alludes to day 41 for anyone who played it, and to nothing for anyone who did not.

## 4. Antagonist

**What exists.** No single antagonist. The system has faces at each level: Pask, who brings "a list of
figures already in it" (`q_world:164`); Holt, who "has a quota of his own, from Arlen, for sacks taken"
(`q_world:206`); Krall, who stands to report 340 "the way you'd say it in church" (`q_office:12`); and
Gedge, the street-level carrier of contempt. Every one of them has a reason. Holt's is his mother
(`q_granary:21`); Gedge's is his father's lost carting (`q_world:194`). The plot's real antagonist is the
number: 310 written over 190 (`q_ned:35`).

**Gaps.** The number cannot be confronted, and a long game needs a person the player can come back to,
argue with and fail to beat. Holt is the best candidate and appears in two scenes and the fight.

**Proposals.**
- Make Holt the recurring human antagonist of era 2, with a scene where he is right. PROPOSAL, Holt to a
  player who read the true figure at the door: "You know what you did tonight? You fed Krall's End for a
  week. I'll have to take it back off Osby. Osby didn't walk eleven mile in the snow, so Osby gets
  nothing. Write that in your book."
- Across eras the antagonist is a role, never a bloodline: whoever holds the figure. The world record
  can make the antagonist the player's own previous character: in a later run, the chalked line under the
  notice (`q_props:122`) is the last character's words, and a constable reads it out as policy.

## 5. Protagonist

**What exists.** The protagonist is a family trade, one generation per era (`HOUSEHOLDS:4-6`). The
slice has two: Ned, who "Keeps the true figures in pencil on his maps and has never shown them to
anyone" (`q_world:134`), and Will, who "Drinks what the post takes off carts" (`q_world:176`). Neither is
a hero; the design forbids hero framing (PITCHES, Decay constraint 5). The choice at the start is made
as a question about the day: "Whose day is it?" (`q_start:7`).

**Gaps.** The two player men are the least distinct voices in the cast (QUOTA-VOICES 11.7: Ned 0.18,
Will 0.24 attribution). The victims carry more personality than the player does. And the protagonist
has no wish of his own beyond getting through the day, which is true to life and thin for 15 hours.

**Proposals.**
- Give each line's protagonist one private want that has nothing to do with the plot. Ned wants to finish
  the map he started in his first year, with every village drawn to scale. Will wants the pass to Wether
  for his family (it exists: `q_dinner:148`, `q_coda:193`). The want is what the system buys them with.
- Tie the want to the cabinet: a Margin note per protagonist that holds the want and pays for it in the
  voices (THEMES section 5 convictions carry over as the design pattern).

## 6. Climax

**What exists.** The slice's climax is the granary door (`q_granary:9-130`), staged on the day's biggest
choice: the figure in hand decides whether talking out exists at all (`q_granary:39`). Every outcome
costs: the talk-out puts "your names on it, not mine" (`q_granary:79`); holding the bar takes a man "to
show for the night" (`q_granary:91`); breaking it leaves "a boy lying very still" (`q_granary:103`). The
emotional climax comes after the action one, at the table a week later (`q_coda:39-49`), which is the
right order.

**Gaps.** Across five eras the plan has two story fights (era 2 door, era 5 clearance, `STORY:113-118`).
At 15 hours, eras 1, 3 and 4 have hinges without a physical climax. Also, the talk-out is the only route
where the player's words change the outcome, so it risks reading as the good ending.

**Proposals.**
- Give eras 1, 3 and 4 a climax that is a held silence, never a fight: era 1, the census table, where
  the player's ancestor either names Ilo or lets Edwin Mott do it; era 3, the allocation riot seen from
  the allocation office window; era 4, the vote on the mill sale with the player's hand in the air or
  not. Each is a single ink choice (pillar 1).
- Keep the talk-out costly in the next era: era 3's allocation board quotes the era 2 talk-out as the
  reason the Furrowe line is "unreliable" and gives its flat to someone else.

## 7. Diction

**What exists.** The slice's diction is its strongest device. Three word habits carry character:
- **"Only."** Ned at the cart: "It's only for the board, Mrs Haldane. It's only so it's written down
  somewhere. It's only four, and it's only till the review" (`q_ned:156`). Ned at the coda: "It's only
  till the spring. It's only the post, Mary" (`q_coda:141`). His father: "I'm only saying. I'm fond of
  them." (`q_dinner:81`). Minimising is the Furrowe inheritance.
- **"Sorry."** Tomas says it four times in one exit: "Two. At the post road. Yes. Thank you. Sorry. Thank
  you." (`q_ned:103`). Mott: "Sorry. That's not your business. Sorry." (`q_ned:41`). The people below
  apologise for taking up the room.
- **Official formula.** "Received and found correct" (`q_ned:70`), "NO GRAIN TO PASS THE DISTRICT
  LINE WITHOUT A PASS" (`q_will:60`), "Order's the order" (`q_will:168`).
- **Anni's refrain:** "I'm only saying it so somebody's said it" (`q_ned:150`, `q_perspective:36`),
  turned in the coda to "I'm only asking so somebody's asked" (`q_coda:47`).

**Gaps.** The per-class and per-faction guide is [07-VOICE-GUIDE.md](07-VOICE-GUIDE.md). The slice has
no Directorate jargon in Pask's mouth (he speaks plainly by design, `q_office:14`), so the bureaucratic
register lives only on paper. The Hesh lexicon (`QUOTA-VOICES 11.2`: Heshen words for water, rain, the
Well and kin when moved) never surfaces: no Heshen word is spoken in the slice.

**Proposals.**
- Make "only" a tracked word across the Furrowe line: era 5's Nan says it at the clip table, and the
  read-back of the full game counts how many times the player's chosen options used it. The count is
  printed once, flat, without comment.
- Let one Heshen word reach the player in era 2 and recur in every era, with its meaning never glossed.
  PROPOSAL: Tesk's prayer for the water ends on a word the Tarn hear as "a sheep with its leg caught"
  (`q_will:13`). In era 5, Mara uses the same word for the last drops in a kettle.

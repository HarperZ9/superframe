# Inferno 8: hostile self-critique

Part of [../10-INFERNO.md](../10-INFERNO.md). This attacks the plan in files 1 to 7 as the critic would,
using the critic's failure modes (`design/critic/03-POLEMIC-AND-AUDIT.md`) and markers
(`design/critic/07-INFERNO.md`, section 3). Each charge ends with what the plan does about it, or with
"open" where it does nothing yet.

## 1. Where it goes edgy for its own sake

1. **Twelve bosses and fourteen traditions is a catalogue.** The author asked for "many more other
   mythical creatures"; the plan answered with a museum. A Minotaur, a Golem, a Hydra, Fafnir, Saturn and
   Erysichthon in one game about a boy in a cell reads as mythology collected for its own sake. The
   critic's rule, two or three fights at hinges, was the stronger answer. **Done:** a run meets only the
   bosses its hinges armed, three or four in era 2 and one or two later. The myths are methods, never
   named on screen. **Open:** whether twelve should be cut to six before any is built. My own cut, if
   forced: Received and Found Correct, Friday, He's With Me, Nearest One, Order, Everybody Says.
2. **Nearest One is the most designed shock in the plan.** A labyrinth whose way out is a child's thread,
   walked to the wicket, is built to land like Spec Ops' white phosphorus. It may be exactly the
   telegraphed shock the audit warns about (mode 6). **Done:** the thread is never shown on a wrist, no
   child is in the maze, and the yield path is always open. **Open:** a playtest with people who do not
   know the design, asking whether the reveal felt like the game's or like the designer's.
3. **Friday's breath.** A child's breath answering each blow is a sound designed to hurt the player. It
   is a hurt the scene has earned (`content/quota/q_dinner.lwd:190`), and it is also the cheapest lever in
   audio. **Done:** it never plays in phase 3, never in a cutscene, never in a trailer.
4. **The Doom repaint.** Devils with threads on a church wall is the strongest image in the faith file and
   the likeliest to be screenshotted out of context as the game's own picture of the Hesh. **Done:** it is
   optional, world-only, base look, and waits for the Christian and Jewish readers. **Open:** it may need
   cutting even if they pass it.
5. **"Intense battle" against the canon.** The design canon says never design engagement mechanics to hold
   attention. The Descent is a climax built to be intense. **Done:** it is once, built only from the run,
   and a run that resolved its lies without force walks it in silence. **Open:** whether a gauntlet at all
   is a concession to genre.

## 2. Where demons make evil supernatural and excuse the humans

1. **The Golem and Frankenstein are alibi myths.** Both are stories of a made thing that gets away from
   its maker. "It got out of control" is what Will would like to say. **Done:** the Golem grows only from
   the player's own blows and never acts on its own; the Figure never harms anyone and only asks. If
   either is ever written to act unbidden, cut it.
2. **Any inner monster is a second cause.** However strict the rules, a player who has fought Will's lie as
   a clay giant can think of the cell as the giant's work. **Done:** the outer world stays human and
   checked (`humanworldcheck`); every boss wears only its liar's face; perpetrators never enter.
   **Open:** a reader test after era 2: ask players who took Tomas to the cells. If anyone answers with a
   boss's name, the design has failed.
3. **The Accuser blames the record.** Variant A makes the perfect record the devil. That risks the
   opposite alibi: the paperwork did it. **Done:** he says every act was the player's and claims none.
   **Open:** it puts the author's mission on trial, which is its point, and it may read as the author's
   mission condemned. The author should judge that, not this file.
4. **Variant C's voice in the head.** "The voice made me" is a mental-illness reading the handling rules
   forbid ("disorders as people, never labels", `design/QUOTA-DESIGN.md:35`). **Done:** the voices are the
   character's own skills, which the game has used since its first build, and they are moral evasions,
   never a symptom. **Open:** a reader with lived experience of hearing voices should see variant C.

## 3. Where it turns preachy

1. **The two names are a thesis.** Every boss is labelled "virtue on top, sin underneath". That is the
   yetzer hara argument, stated twelve times. **Done:** the sin label is never shown. **Open:** a player
   who learns the pattern by boss three will predict the rest, and prediction is preaching.
2. **The naming verb is a quiz with a right answer.** Choosing "what happened" over "what was told" is a
   moral test the player can pass. **Done:** naming costs standing under attack, changes no fate, and
   gives nothing. **Open:** passing it still feels good. The plan has no answer to that yet.
3. **The disclaimer states the theme.** "I never made you do anything. I asked, and you answered." is the
   devil saying the game's argument aloud, which the allegory audit forbids for any character
   (`design/literary/06-ALLEGORY-AUDIT.md:10`). **Done:** it is said once per playthrough, in one line,
   with no music. **Open:** it may need to be cut to its act: he shows the player's hand and says nothing.
4. **Non-force outcomes still pay.** Yielding raises a lie's `dc` and often raises CONDOLENCE. That is a
   reward for the good choice, the virtue economy the critic warned of (`design/critic/06-BOSSES.md`,
   section 2.4). **Done:** each non-force outcome also costs something: standing, footing in the next
   fight, waking with nothing changed. **Open:** the `dc` gain is a gain. A harder version gives the
   non-force outcomes no mechanical change at all, only the scene that follows. The author should choose.

## 4. Where it takes what is not ours

1. **Borrowed sacred figures as game content.** Iblis, Mara, the Golem and ha-satan belong to living
   faiths. Using their structures for boss mechanics is a form of mythic tourism, however the iconography
   is avoided. **Done:** no sacred name in the world by default; no prophet; readers for each tradition
   used; the Wendigo declined. **Open:** the Golem's inversion, a protector made by the persecuting side,
   may itself be an offence to the tradition it borrows from. The Jewish reader decides.
2. **The author's word "shaytan".** The plan declines to put it in the world without the author's
   decision. That overrides the author's phrasing on a judgement call, and says so ([05-FAITH.md](05-FAITH.md)
   section 4).

## 5. What the plan cannot fix by design

1. **Only perpetrators get the spectacle.** The inner arena belongs to the Furrowe and Brack lines. The
   Hesh have scenes and no arena (08-ARENA rule 6). The most elaborate system in the game is spent on the
   people who did the harm. The rule is right, because a victim's suffering must not become anyone's
   growth arena. The cost is real: the game's craft budget follows its perpetrators.
2. **A fighting game that punishes fighting.** The arena has real depth (`PROGRESS.md`, steps 154 to 156).
   A player who enjoys it gets the lie proved every time. That is the design's point and it risks the
   player feeling tricked, the way some felt about Undertale's moral binary. The plan offers no middle:
   force is never right inside the inner arena. The external yard keeps fighting honest.

## 6. What keeps it honest

- The rules in [02-BESTIARY-A.md](02-BESTIARY-A.md) section 1, each one a check in [06-SYSTEMS.md](06-SYSTEMS.md)
  section 6 with planted controls that must fail.
- No fate is touched, no tally is kept, no boon is free, and every boon's price lands on a named person.
- The outer world stays human, and a check enforces it.
- The grey test and the trailer test, before anything is built.
- Readers from every tradition borrowed, paid, before content.
- The Adversary is not picked here. The author picks it, and the critic's objections to each variant stand
  next to it in [04-ADVERSARY.md](04-ADVERSARY.md) section 5.

# Critic 1: mechanisms, film, television and documentary

Part of [../CRITIC.md](../CRITIC.md). Twenty works. For each: the setup and the mechanism that makes it
land, what it refuses the audience, what it costs the audience, and what Low Water could take as method.
Content is never taken. Facts are from memory at high confidence unless marked **verified** (a web
source checked on 2026-10-22, listed in the index) or **UNVERIFIED**.

The scenes are described plainly. Nothing here re-enacts them.

---

### 1. Come and See (Elem Klimov, 1985)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Flyora, a boy who digs a rifle out of the sand to join the partisans, is
  aged by what he sees until his face is an old man's. The centre is a German unit herding a Belarusian
  village into a barn and burning it, filmed as a long event with music, laughter and photographs taken
  by the perpetrators. The mechanism is duration and the body: the camera stays on faces looking into the
  lens, sound goes to tinnitus after a shelling, and the massacre is a social occasion for the killers.
- **Refuses.** A cut away. A hero. Any victory. At the end Flyora shoots at a portrait of Hitler while
  archive footage runs backward to a baby photograph, and he stops at the baby and does not fire.
- **Cost.** The viewer is made to stand in the crowd at the barn for as long as the boy does.
- **Method for Low Water.** The face as the record: let a child's face carry the elapsed horror instead
  of a document. Let perpetrators enjoy themselves in public, as a party. The reverse-footage ending is a
  method for era 5: run the town's history backward until it reaches a child, and refuse the shot.

### 2. Grave of the Fireflies (Isao Takahata, 1988)
- **Setup and mechanism.** The first line tells you the narrator, Seita, died in a train station, and
  his ghost meets his little sister Setsuko's. Every scene after that is a slow walk toward two deaths
  already announced. The mechanism is foreknowledge plus pride: Seita leaves the aunt's house rather than
  bow to her, and that choice, made by a teenage boy who loves his sister, is what starves her.
- **Refuses.** Suspense, and a villain. The aunt is unkind and right about the food. The firebombing is
  the weather.
- **Cost.** You watch a child's small joys (a tin of fruit drops, fireflies in a cave) knowing they are
  counting down, and you cannot blame the war alone.
- **Method.** Announce the death first. "He went to the hills" can be on the first screen of era 2, and
  the player spends the day watching the people who make it true. The failing carer is a child who loves.

### 3. Threads (Mick Jackson, writer Barry Hines, BBC, 1984)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Sheffield before, during and for thirteen years after a nuclear exchange,
  shot like a documentary with flat on-screen captions of casualty figures and fallout data. The
  mechanism is the caption against the image: the flat information voice makes the pictures worse.
- **Refuses.** Rescue, a recovery arc, and an ending. It closes on a freeze frame of a girl's face as she
  sees the baby she has just delivered, and cuts before her scream.
- **Cost.** Hope is removed in stages, each stage dated.
- **Method.** The flat voice works when it is set against a face and stops before the reaction. This is
  the strongest defence of the old two-column read-back, and Low Water dropped it on the author's
  direction. Keep the method for one moment only, not as the ending's form (see the audit, 03).

### 4. The Zone of Interest (Jonathan Glazer, 2023)
- **Setup and mechanism.** The Auschwitz commandant's family lives in a house beside the camp wall: a
  garden, a pool, children's birthdays. The camp is never shown. It is heard: shots, screams, trains,
  furnaces, under the family's ordinary talk. The mechanism is sound against picture and the banality of
  a domestic plot (the wife does not want to move house).
- **Refuses.** Any look over the wall. Any character who objects in a way that matters.
- **Cost.** The viewer becomes the family's habit of not hearing. Near the end the film cuts forward to
  present-day museum cleaners vacuuming the exhibits, and back to the commandant retching on a staircase.
- **Method.** Put the player in a house beside the cells and keep the cells off screen while the sound
  stays on. László Nemes's **Son of Saul** (2015) is the companion method: a 4:3 frame locked on one
  prisoner's face with the killing out of focus behind him, and a plot about one man trying to give one
  boy a proper burial. Both bear on Tomas, whose burial was never recorded.

### 5. The Act of Killing (Joshua Oppenheimer, 2012) and The Look of Silence (2014)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Anwar Congo, a celebrated killer from the 1965 to 1966 Indonesian killings,
  re-enacts his murders for the camera in film genres he loves. Perpetrators boast. Near the end, on the
  rooftop where he strangled people with wire, he retches and cannot stop. The companion film follows
  Adi, an optometrist born after his brother Ramli was killed, who tests the eyes of the men responsible
  while asking them about it (**verified**: Adi born after the killing, meets killers under the guise of
  eye tests).
- **Refuses.** A tribunal. The killers are still powerful and still proud; nobody arrests anyone.
- **Cost.** Laughing with a killer before you understand the joke. Watching a brother ask questions to
  men who would kill him again.
- **Method.** The perpetrator re-enacts with pride, and the body confesses before the mouth. The
  younger brother born into the absence is exactly Aaron's position in era 3, and the eye test is a form
  for a witness scene that is also a service (he is helping them see while they describe the killing).

### 6. Shoah (Claude Lanzmann, 1985)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Nine hours with no archive footage, only places as they are now and people
  speaking. Abraham Bomba, a Treblinka survivor who was forced to cut women's hair in the gas chamber,
  is filmed in a barbershop Lanzmann rented, cutting a friend's hair while he talks. He reaches the
  women of his own town, breaks off, and Lanzmann presses him to go on (**verified**: the rented shop,
  the friend in the chair, the breakdown; the exact words of the pressing are not confirmed and are not
  quoted here).
- **Refuses.** Images of the dead. Consolation. Any let-off for the witness.
- **Cost.** The viewer watches a man made to keep talking and is complicit in the pressing.
- **Method.** Testimony through the hands doing the old work. Anni at forty laying bowls while she
  talks is already this; the witness plan should let her work and fail to finish a sentence, and should
  put someone in the room who presses her.

### 7. Funny Games (Michael Haneke, 1997; US remake 2007)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Two polite young men torment a family at a lake house. One of them talks to
  the camera. When the mother shoots one, the other picks up a remote and rewinds the film, and the
  shot never happens. The child is killed off screen while a killer makes a sandwich in the kitchen.
- **Refuses.** Catharsis. Revenge. The genre's promised turn.
- **Cost.** The film accuses the viewer of wanting the violence it withholds.
- **Method.** Low Water's form rule forbids a fourth-wall break (`design/sensory/03-SURREAL.md:37`). The
  rewind can be done inside the fiction: a record that unwrites an act the player saw (the granary talk-out
  rewritten in the next era's Gazette as a riot put down). The player saw it, and the world says otherwise.

### 8. Oldboy (Park Chan-wook, 2003)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Oh Dae-su is imprisoned for fifteen years without explanation, released,
  and led to his captor. The reveal is that the young woman he has fallen in love with is his daughter,
  and that the whole design was revenge for a piece of schoolboy gossip that led the captor's sister to
  her death. Dae-su cuts out his own tongue.
- **Refuses.** Proportion. The cause is tiny and the punishment total.
- **Cost.** The viewer's sympathy for the hero is used as the trap.
- **Method.** The cause is a careless sentence spoken years earlier. In Low Water the candidate is a
  child's sentence: Davey's "Is he in the cells? Can I come and see?" (`content/quota/q_dinner.lwd:164`)
  or Pell's repeated teacher talk, returning a generation later with its weight.

### 9. Caché (Michael Haneke, 2005)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A television presenter receives surveillance tapes of his own house. The
  trail leads to Majid, an Algerian man whose parents were killed in the Paris massacre of 17 October
  1961 and whom the presenter's family meant to adopt. As a six-year-old the presenter lied about Majid,
  and Majid was sent to an orphanage. When they meet as adults Majid cuts his own throat in front of him.
- **Refuses.** Who sent the tapes. Any apology.
- **Cost.** The child's lie is real persecution, small and domestic, and the adult refuses to own it.
- **Method.** A child's lie about a child of the persecuted people, made inside a household, which the
  liar carries as a forgotten nothing. This is the cleanest model for era 2's children (Pell, Davey).

### 10. Dancer in the Dark (Lars von Trier, 2000)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Selma, a nearly blind factory worker, escapes into musical numbers in her
  head. She is executed by hanging, singing, and the song is cut off by the drop.
- **Refuses.** A reprieve, which the film builds toward.
- **Cost.** The form the viewer has learned to use as relief becomes the place of the death.
- **Method.** If Low Water teaches the player a refuge form (the dream, the surreal break, the arena),
  it must once be the room where the worst thing happens.

### 11. Who Can Kill a Child? (Narciso Ibáñez Serrador, 1976)
- **Setup and mechanism.** The film opens with about eight minutes of archive footage of children as
  victims of war (camps, Korea, Vietnam, Biafra, India and Pakistan) (**verified**), then cuts to a
  holiday couple on an island where the children have killed the adults.
- **Refuses.** An explanation of the children. A safe adult.
- **Cost.** The prologue makes the fiction an indictment, and then asks the viewer to want a child shot.
- **Method.** The documentary prologue that turns a genre into a charge. Low Water's era 5 can open on
  the clip and its comments, and then make the player handle a child of the next people as a threat.

### 12. Twin Peaks: The Return, Part 8 (David Lynch and Mark Frost, 2017)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Midway through a crime series, an hour leaves the plot for the Trinity test
  of 16 July 1945, set to Penderecki's *Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima*, then a near-silent 1956
  desert where soot-black men stalk a radio station and one repeats a chant into the microphone that puts
  listeners to sleep. A girl swallows a winged insect as she sleeps.
- **Refuses.** Explanation, plot, and the series' own tone.
- **Cost.** The viewer loses the frame they came with, and the episode makes an origin of evil out of a
  real weapon.
- **Method.** One whole chapter that leaves the protagonists for the origin, in a different form. For
  Low Water: an era 1 chapter with no player character, only the census table and a song.

### 13. The Red Wedding (George R. R. Martin, *A Storm of Swords*, 2000; *Game of Thrones* 3.9, 2013)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A young king who broke a marriage pledge attends a wedding to make amends.
  Guest right, a sacred law of hospitality, is broken: the band plays a song the audience has learned to
  hear as a threat, a mother notices armour under a host's sleeve, and the king, his mother and his
  followers are killed at the feast.
- **Refuses.** The genre contract that the hero's line survives.
- **Cost.** The viewer's trust in the story's protection.
- **Method.** A sacred rule of the table is broken at the table. Low Water's table is its holiest place.
  One meal at a Haldane table should be the trap, with the clue in a song the player knows.

### 14. Breaking Bad, "Phoenix" (2.12, 2009)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Walter White stands over Jane, his partner's girlfriend, as she chokes in her
  sleep. He moves to help, stops, and watches her die. Her death later leads, through her grieving
  father, to an air disaster.
- **Refuses.** A struggle, a motive spoken aloud, and an act. It is an omission.
- **Cost.** The viewer sees the moment of choice and the protagonist's face deciding not to act.
- **Method.** The worst act is doing nothing within reach. "Say nothing" at the post
  (`content/quota/q_ned.lwd:179`) already is this; it needs the camera on the player's face, and a
  consequence that travels far through a grieving father.

### 15. The Wire (David Simon, HBO, 2002 to 2008)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Wallace, a teenager in the drug trade who looks after younger children, is
  shot by his two closest friends on their boss's order (season 1). In season 4, Randy, a schoolboy who
  told the police what he knew, is exposed by a careless officer as a snitch; his foster home is
  firebombed and he ends in a group home.
- **Refuses.** Individual villains. The officers who exposed Randy meant well.
- **Cost.** Kindness by an institution kills a child's future.
- **Method.** The institution's kindness as the instrument. In Low Water, the inspector's notebook
  and his welcome of Tomas are the same kind of careless care.

### Weighed and cut
- **Psycho (1960).** The star killed at the end of the first act. Madoka (file 02) carries the same
  mechanism with a closer fit, a children's form.
- **Requiem for a Dream (2000)** and **Audition (1999).** The accelerating montage and the tonal pivot
  from romance to torture. Come and See's duration and Madoka's whiplash cover both, and neither bears on
  a persecution story.
- **Dear Zachary (Kurt Kuenne, 2008)**, from memory, high: a film made as a letter to a dead man's
  infant son, whose form changes when the child is killed by his mother. A document whose addressee dies
  during its making. Weighed for the witness scene; not used, because Anni's listener lives.

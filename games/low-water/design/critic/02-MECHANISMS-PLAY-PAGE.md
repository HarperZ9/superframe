# Critic 2: mechanisms, games, anime and manga, comics, literature and theatre

Part of [../CRITIC.md](../CRITIC.md). Twenty-three entries. Same four questions as file 01. Memory at
high confidence unless marked **verified** or **UNVERIFIED**.

## Games

### 1. Spec Ops: The Line (Yager, 2012)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A squad in a ruined Dubai. To pass a defended position the player fires
  white phosphorus mortars by marking targets on a thermal camera feed, white shapes on a screen. Walking
  through afterwards, the player finds the burned bodies of civilians the soldiers were sheltering,
  including a mother holding a child. The game gives no way to refuse and continue. Loading screens
  later address the player in second person with lines in the vein of "Do you feel like a hero yet?"
- **Refuses.** The option to be good. The game's only way forward is the war crime.
- **Cost.** The player's hands are on the act; the reflection of the player character's face is in the
  targeting screen.
- **Method.** Abstraction as the weapon: the screen that makes people into shapes is the instrument of
  the crime. Low Water's instrument is the sheet and the figure. The figure the player writes should once
  be shown as what it did, at the place, with the abstraction stripped. Forced complicity is a tool to
  use once, where the fiction's chain of command makes refusal impossible for this person.

### 2. BioShock (Irrational Games, 2007)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A friendly radio guide has prefaced every instruction with "would you
  kindly". Andrew Ryan reveals the phrase is a conditioning trigger, and that the player character has
  obeyed it all game. He orders the player to kill him with a golf club, saying a man chooses and a slave
  obeys. Control is taken away for the killing.
- **Refuses.** The player's belief that following the objective was choice.
- **Cost.** Every quest marker followed becomes evidence against the player.
- **Method.** A politeness formula planted early as texture and later revealed as command. Low Water
  has the formula already: "Received and found correct" and "only". The order on the nail
  (`content/quota/q_will.lwd:60`) can be the trigger the player has obeyed all day.

### 3. Mouthwashing (Wrong Organ, 2024)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A freighter crew stranded after a crash. Scenes before the crash are shown
  through the captain, Curly; scenes after through the co-pilot, Jimmy, who blames Curly. The late reveal
  is that Jimmy crashed the ship deliberately in a failed murder-suicide, that he had sexually assaulted
  the nurse Anya, who later dies of an overdose, and that the maimed captain is his scapegoat
  (**verified**, Wikipedia plot summary; one critic's account names the assault as the core of the
  reveal). Fake crash glitches make the player think the game has crashed.
- **Refuses.** A trustworthy viewpoint. The player has been inside the perpetrator's self-account.
- **Cost.** The player's sympathy for Jimmy's exhaustion and grievance is retroactively his alibi.
- **Method.** Two viewpoints that disagree, and the late discovery that one was a self-serving story.
  The self-lie system is this already; it needs one scene where the player's own character's viewpoint is
  revealed as the edit, by a scene from before.

### 4. OMORI (OMOCAT, 2020)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A bright dream world of friends, picnics and a cheerful hero. The truth,
  reached through returning images (a violin, a staircase, a hanging shape), is that Sunny pushed his
  sister Mari down the stairs in a quarrel, killing her, and that he and his friend Basil staged her body
  as a hanging to hide it. The dream world exists to not know.
- **Refuses.** The truth until the player chooses to face it; a route lets the player keep the dream.
- **Cost.** The cuteness is the cover story, and the player has enjoyed it.
- **Method.** A soft surface over a buried act, and a cover-up done by two children together. "He went
  to the hills" is the Haldane version; Bet and Anni can be the two who stage it.

### 5. Silent Hill 2 (Konami, 2001)
- **Setup and mechanism.** James comes to a town because of a letter from his dead wife. Monsters are
  shaped by his guilt and desire; the executioner figure punishes him because he wants punishment. A
  videotape in a hotel room shows that he smothered Mary with a pillow as she lay ill.
- **Refuses.** An external villain. The monsters are his.
- **Cost.** The player has spent the game helping a man find the woman he killed.
- **Method.** The world is shaped by what the protagonist will not admit, and the proof is a record of
  his own act found late. See file 06 for the bosses.

### 6. Shadow of the Colossus (Team Ico, 2005)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A young man kills sixteen great creatures to revive a girl. Most do not
  attack until attacked. Each death plays mournful music, and he grows darker, horned, corrupted.
- **Refuses.** Any confirmation that the quest is good.
- **Cost.** The player's skill is the instrument of the harm, and the better you play, the sooner the
  innocent thing dies.
- **Method.** Mastery as complicity. Directly relevant to the arena (file 06).

### 7. NieR: Automata (PlatinumGames, 2017) and Undertale (Toby Fox, 2015)
- **Setup and mechanism.** In NieR's final ending the player can delete their own save data to help a
  stranger finish. In Undertale's genocide route the player kills every creature, and the game remembers
  across resets, so a later "perfect" ending is altered for good.
- **Refuses.** The player's assumption that the save file is outside the fiction.
- **Cost.** Something the player owns.
- **Method.** Low Water's world record is this lever, and the current design spends it on gifts (a
  stone with a name). It should once cost the player: a line they wrote kept against their will (file
  05, proposal 13).

### 8. Metal Gear Solid 3: Snake Eater (Konami, 2004), The Sorrow
- **Setup and mechanism.** A boss fight that is a walk upstream through a river, past the ghost of
  every person the player killed in the game so far, in order. The length of the walk is the player's
  body count.
- **Refuses.** Combat. You cannot hurt the Sorrow.
- **Cost.** A quiet walk measured in the player's own past acts.
- **Method.** The game's memory of the player's acts as a place to walk through. For Low Water it is the
  burial ground's corner, walked in era 5 along the verge of the ring road.

### 9. Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons (Starbreeze, 2013)
- **Setup and mechanism.** One controller, one stick and trigger per brother. The younger cannot swim
  and rides his brother's back through water. When the older brother dies, the younger must cross water
  alone, and the input that crosses it is the dead brother's trigger (**verified**, player accounts and
  a 2024 remake review).
- **Refuses.** A cutscene. The player does the grief with their hand.
- **Cost.** The player's muscle memory of the dead.
- **Method.** A verb that belonged to the dead child, given to the living one. Tomas's verb in the
  slice is speaking to officials for the family. Aaron "says nothing to anyone in uniform"
  (`content/quota/q_world.lwd:224`). In era 3 Aaron's first line to a uniform uses Tomas's verb.

### 10. Papers, Please (Lucas Pope, 2013)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A border inspector checks documents for pay that heats his flat and feeds
  his family. Entrants plead; a woman's husband is admitted and she is not; the rules change each day.
- **Refuses.** Time to care. Every kindness costs your family's food on the end-of-day screen.
- **Cost.** The player starts to stamp faster and look less.
- **Method.** The nearest game to Ned. Its lesson for Low Water is pace: a sequence of many small
  cases, each a person, under a clock, until the player stops reading faces. The returns room needs it.

### 11. Pathologic (Ice-Pick Lodge, 2005; Pathologic 2, 2019)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A plague in an isolated steppe town over twelve days; three healers see the
  same events from different viewpoints (**verified**). The player cannot save everyone and loses named
  people while hungry and short of time. Children run their own fortress, the Polyhedron (**verified**).
  The town's framing as a staged play with masked actors who comment on the player is from memory,
  moderate (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Refuses.** Enough time. Every day you choose whom not to visit.
- **Cost.** People die because you were elsewhere, and you learn it the next morning.
- **Method.** Scarcity of the player's own time as the moral resource. Era 2's day already runs on a
  clock; the four days after the arrest should too (file 04, proposal 3).

### 12. Fear and Hunger (Miro Haverinen, 2020)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A dungeon crawler where many outcomes, including saving and escaping instant
  death, turn on a coin toss the player calls, and where limbs can be lost in and out of combat
  (**verified**, Wikipedia; critics' accounts).
- **Refuses.** Fairness. The player's planning can be undone by chance.
- **Cost.** The body is fragile and the world is indifferent.
- **Method.** Chance as the regime's face. In a famine the arbitrary is the policy: who stood nearest
  the wicket (`content/quota/q_granary.lwd:91`). Keep the arbitrariness in the fiction; never give the
  player the coin, because a coin lets the regime off.

### 13. Disco Elysium (ZA/UM, 2019): the Deserter and the Pale
- **Setup and mechanism.** A murder case solved, late, on an island fort: the killer is an old deserter
  of a lost revolution who has watched the town through a rifle scope for decades and shot the victim
  out of jealousy and hatred. The case's political meaning shrinks to one bitter old man. The Pale, a grey
  nothing between the isles that erodes memory and matter, is the world's slow end.
- **Refuses.** A satisfying culprit. The ideology the game has dramatised ends in a small, sour act.
- **Cost.** The player's investment in the case's meaning.
- **Method.** The ideological struggle reaches its end in one embittered person. The Pale is a model for
  era 5's forgetting: an erosion with a physics.

## Anime, manga and comics

### 14. The End of Evangelion (Hideaki Anno, 1997)
- **Setup and mechanism.** The finale of a giant-robot series becomes the end of humanity, with live
  action footage, a theatre audience, and on-screen messages addressed to the director, some hostile
  (**verified** in outline; one fan wiki reports that the staff wrote the letters rather than using real
  ones, which this file treats as reported, not settled).
- **Refuses.** The genre ending, and the boundary between the film and its audience.
- **Cost.** The viewer sees themselves in the seats.
- **Method.** Ruled out as a fourth-wall break by Low Water's surreal rule
  (`design/sensory/03-SURREAL.md:37`). The audit (file 03) challenges that rule.

### 15. Berserk, the Eclipse (Kentaro Miura, manga, 1990s)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Griffith, the beloved leader of a mercenary band, after a year of torture,
  sacrifices his whole band to demons to become one of them. Guts watches his comrades eaten and the
  woman he loves, Casca, sexually assaulted by the transformed Griffith.
- **Refuses.** The leader's love. The band's loyalty is the price paid.
- **Cost.** The reader admired Griffith's ambition for volumes.
- **Method.** The beloved leader's dream, admired by the reader, is the instrument. In Low Water the
  admired dream is the honest figure or the read-aloud; one era should show what an admired leader of
  the Hesh or of the honest faction sold to keep it.

### 16. Devilman (Go Nagai, 1972 to 1973)
- **Setup and mechanism.** As fear of demons spreads, ordinary neighbours form mobs to find them among
  themselves. Miki, the hero's loving girlfriend, is murdered by a mob that suspects her family, and her
  head is displayed on a pike. The hero's best friend is revealed as Satan.
- **Refuses.** Any decency in the crowd.
- **Cost.** The friendly neighbourhood is the killer.
- **Method.** The scapegoating mob made of the cast. The granary crowd in Low Water is victims; the
  persecuting crowd needs the same faces.

### 17. Puella Magi Madoka Magica (Shaft, 2011), episode 3
- **Setup and mechanism.** A cute magical-girl show. In episode three the confident mentor is killed by
  a witch in one bite, mid-fight, at her happiest.
- **Refuses.** The form's protection of its girls.
- **Cost.** The viewer's genre literacy.
- **Method.** Tonal whiplash timed to a character's peak joy. The joy-before-the-hinge proposals must
  place the blow inside the joy.

### 18. Maus (Art Spiegelman, 1980 to 1991)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A son records his father's Holocaust survival. The father burned the son's
  mother's wartime diaries after her suicide, and the son calls him a murderer. In the last pages the
  dying father calls the son by the name of the brother who died in the war, Richieu, asking to stop
  for now (**verified**: the misnaming, the request to stop). The last image is a shared headstone.
- **Refuses.** Reconciliation. The book ends on a slip of the tongue.
- **Cost.** The living son has been the dead son's replacement all along.
- **Method.** The single most useful mechanism for Low Water's spine. A parent who calls the living
  child by the dead child's name. Bet calling Aaron "Tomas" once, in era 3. Ilo burning a paper, and a
  child naming it what it is.

## Literature

### 19. Blood Meridian (Cormac McCarthy, 1985)
- **Setup and mechanism.** A scalp-hunting gang on the Mexican border, narrated in a flat, biblical
  catalogue of killing. The Judge, a learned giant, dances at the end in a saloon after an act in an
  outhouse the novel does not describe, and he says he will never die.
- **Refuses.** Interior life, judgement, and any frame that names the violence as wrong.
- **Cost.** The reader has to supply the horror because the prose will not.
- **Method.** The unjudging catalogue and the offstage act. The ellipsis at the outhouse is the model
  for Tomas's last night.

### 20. Beloved (Toni Morrison, 1987) and Sophie's Choice (William Styron, 1979)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Sethe, who escaped slavery, killed her baby daughter when the slavers came,
  so the child would not be taken back; the act is approached from several angles before it is told.
  Sophie, at Auschwitz, was made by a doctor to choose which of her two children would die; the novel
  holds this back until late.
- **Refuses.** A verdict on the mother.
- **Cost.** The reader cannot condemn or excuse and must hold both.
- **Method.** The parent's choice, delayed and approached sideways. File 04, proposal 4, uses it.

### 21. Never Let Me Go (Kazuo Ishiguro, 2005) and "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (Ursula K. Le Guin, 1973)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Clones raised at a gentle school to give their organs never rebel; the
  rumour of a deferral for lovers is false. Omelas's happiness depends on one child kept in a cellar,
  and every citizen is shown the child.
- **Refuses.** Rebellion. Both stories deny the reader the escape plot.
- **Cost.** The reader's own acceptance of the arrangement.
- **Method.** The calm, kind arrangement that runs on one child. The town's relief works this way.

### 22. Night (Elie Wiesel, 1956 and 1958)
- **Setup and mechanism.** In a camp the SS hang a boy with two men in front of the assembled prisoners;
  the boy is light and dies slowly, and a man behind the narrator asks where God is.
- **Refuses.** Any meaning made from the boy's death.
- **Cost.** The reader is in the assembly that is made to watch, and then marched past.
- **Method.** A child's death in custody watched by the people made to file past it. The opposite of
  Low Water's current design, where nobody watches.

## Theatre

### 23. Antigone (Sophocles, about 441 BCE)
- **Setup and mechanism.** Creon forbids burial of a traitor; his sister buries him and is walled up
  alive. Creon relents too late and loses his son and his wife.
- **Refuses.** A compromise. Both principles destroy the house.
- **Cost.** The audience sides with Antigone and watches the state's law win the day and lose the house.
- **Method.** The denied burial as the whole drama. The Hesh bury with water
  (`design/quota/01-WORLD.md:43`); Tomas was put in clay by the man who arrested him
  (`content/quota/q_lore.lwd:73-76`). Somebody in a later era must try to bury him properly and pay.

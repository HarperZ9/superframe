One in the afternoon. The light through the planking is yellow and moves, because the clouds are moving, fast, all one way.

The coat on its peg has two tide marks at the hem now, one above the other.

Something is coming up the coast. The knees knew before the Glass did.

Nine hundred and sixty-two, darling, and the bottom's not in sight. Tonight the wind will want everything that isn't nailed down, and some things that are.

Gullet in colour for the last time this week, and busy. Shutters going up over the oxblood and the mustard. Sabine and Teague are filling sacks with sand from a cart. Somebody on a ladder is roping the Brine's sign to the Brine.

Out on the mole, small and dark against a sky the colour of a slate, a woman in an oxblood coat stands looking at the lamp.

Walk out along the mole to Wenna.

Go to the tube, where Pip will be.

Go down to the boats, where they are making fast.

Go into the Brine, where the engineer is packing.

Go back up and sleep until the wind.

The spray is already coming over the rail in single sheets, a long time apart.

She'll want a hand at the panel all night. Every time the board trips, and it will trip.

My knees'll do the stairs once. Not twice. Not three times.

She does not look at the assessor. She looks at the lamp.

Say you'll sit with her, if it comes to that.

Say nothing, and look at the lamp too.

If it comes to that.

It'll come to that.

She puts something in the assessor's hand without looking: a brass key on a string, warm from her neck.

They look at the lamp together until a sheet of spray reaches them both. Neither steps back.

You'd do. If you had to.

Pip is on her crate by the brass mouth with the afternoon canister in her lap, unopened.

Can a person keep a promise for somebody that's gone? For a company that's gone, even?

She turns the canister over. The cap is dented from years of being dropped onto the same hook.

Ask her who she means.

Tell her a promise kept is a promise kept, whoever it was made to.

Nobody. Somebody I know. Forget I said.

She hangs the canister on its hook without reading it, which, she told the assessor on the first night, is the shilling.

A promise kept is kept. Whoever it was made to.

That's what she says.

Pip hears what she has said a moment after she has said it, and goes red to the ears, in daylight, where everyone can see.

At the mole steps Merrow is doubling his lines, and then doubling the doubles. His boat rides low and clean.

Eye comes over about a quarter past twelve. Forty minutes, if the Glass is honest. Forty minutes and you could row to the Old Light and back and have time to look.

He holds out the end of a line.

Take it, and make it fast to the ring.

Leave the line to him.

The assessor makes the line fast. Merrow undoes the knot without comment and makes it again, the way it is made.

Boat's here at midnight. Whoever wants it.

Lugg is making the Company's boats fast alone. He works bent and careful, not looking up.

Eye comes over at a quarter past twelve. Tub would go. Tub always goes.

Hold the bow while he ties it.

Leave him to it.

The assessor holds the bow. Lugg ties it, and the next one, and then stands with his hand on the gunwale as if it were somebody's shoulder.

Boat's here at midnight.

The engineer has his specifications spread on the Brine's table to dry. He is packing them into the satchel in an order that matters to him.

The coast road will be closed by four. I shall be stuck, assessor. I find I don't mind.

Help him put the pages in order.

Leave him to his satchel.

The assessor hands him the pages by number. He takes each one as if it might be the last. Then one is.

Thank you. I am sorry about the adjectives.

The assessor lies down in the moving yellow light and listens to the town nail itself shut.

At half past three the light goes the colour of a bruise, and then the colour of nothing, and the wind arrives at the end of the mole like a person who has run all the way.

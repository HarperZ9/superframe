The wind comes round the Spit and finds the town. Every loose thing in Gullet tries to leave at once.

The wind stops. Not drops: stops, the way a held breath stops. The rain stops with it. Over Gullet, in a round hole in the cloud, every star there is.

The wind comes back from the north-west, harder, as if it had only gone round to fetch something.

Far out along the mole, through the rain, Mother's beam stops in the middle of a sweep, as if a hand had been put over it. It does not come back.

At the top of the quay steps the paraffin post goes out, not in a gust: all at once, the way a lamp goes out when somebody turns it down.

On the weigh-house roof the lamp on the town balance goes out all at once. A slate comes down into the street a moment later.

Up on Chancel Hill the white lamps go out together, which they only do when somebody throws the second switch.

In the gasholder frame the Old Bulb's light goes out, and the frame goes on singing in the dark.

Out at the bay's mouth the collier's lights turn, slowly, the way a large animal turns, and line up on the leading lights the town has kept. They come in through the dog-leg one after another, and the Spit is behind them.

Out at the bay's mouth the collier's lights come on toward the town, steering for a lamp that is not there. Then they stop moving, and lean.

The water is over the quay's edge and coming. Somebody is ringing the Brine's bell, the one over the bar that nobody has rung since Jory, and the whole town is going toward it.

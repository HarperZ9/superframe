Tobit Quill is asleep under a table with his bare feet stuck out toward the fire, and nobody has the heart to move them. Nan Cockle, at the front of the queue, gives up her place without being asked and without getting up.

When I was a girl the Old Light had a door, and a stove inside it, and the keeper let you warm your feet on his dog. Good dog. Very flat by the end.

Twenty minutes. Tuppence. The assessor's coat now smells of the Brine, which is to say of paraffin, vinegar, wet wool and everyone.

The small hours. The Brine has gone quiet the way a full room does when it is too tired to leave. Nan Cockle snores on the settle, regular and seaworthy. Penhallow sits by a porthole with her cap on her knee, watching the mole.

The nearest door with heat behind it is Shadwell's: a tall black room hung to the roof with herring in their hundreds, all looking down, all gold, over a bed of oak dust that smoulders and does not flame.

In. Shut it. Every time that door opens the herring lose ten minutes.

Ned Shadwell does not turn round. He is looking up at the third beam, where the soot has a sheen on it like a beetle's back.

Twenty past something, by the soot. The beam doesn't do hours. Clocks do hours, and clocks have faces, and a face will lie. Soot's got nothing to be vain about.

Twenty minutes in the smoke. Afterwards the coat smells of oak, and the lane outside, for the first time all night, of nothing in particular.

Shadwell's smokehouse is a tall black room hung to the roof with gold herring. By the fire, on an upturned crate that is not equal to him, sits Marrow Dunlin, his blindfold still round his neck, eating a kipper whole from the tail up.

He's been here since the soot was on the second beam. He's eaten eleven.

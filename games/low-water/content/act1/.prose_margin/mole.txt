The assessor goes over the lee rail onto the weed ledge and passes him in the dark a yard below his boots, and is up the hundred and twelve before he reaches the door. The lamp room is empty, and frosting.

A boot on the ledge goes into the sea to the knee. Above, the harbourmaster stops and looks down over the rail.

The Board can hear you, assessor. The Board has been wet for eleven years and has an excellent ear for feet.

He walks on, faster.

The assessor throws salt ahead on the white stone and walks on grey, and up the stairs the same, a fist of salt to every turn, and empties the last of the sack round the foot of the lens.

Grit underfoot. Lovely, honest grit.

The assessor runs the rope through the stair door's iron handle and round the gallery rail post twice, and pulls it tight enough to sing.

The assessor salts the floor round the lens in a wide grey ring. Whatever he drips on that will not hold.

The assessor goes out onto the gallery, pulls the door to, and crouches by the frosted glass where the lens is a grey shape and the stair door can be seen through the ferns.

The cold on the gallery is the cold of being outside a window. Far down, a boot. A drip.

He stands there a long time. Then he takes his hand off the glass and goes down, one drip per step, and never knows the Shelf was outside the window.

The gallery door opens on its frozen hinge. He turns, with his hand still on the lens and his own words still in the room.

The Board was speaking privately. The Board would like that minuted.

The assessor comes in low across his ice and closes both hands on the haft beside his. He lets go at once, as a man lets go of something hot, and turns, and sees who it is.

The gallery door shrieks on its frozen hinge. He turns with it in his hand.

The assessor stays by the glass. He takes his hand off the lens, and steps back, and lifts it in both hands.

A boot on the top step. A drip. The handle of the stair door turns, and the rope takes the strain, and sings.

Assessor. The Board perceives that its door has been barred. The Board would be glad to know by whom, though the Board has a theory, and the theory is wet.

You climbed in a frost that keeps every drip, with a claim you sent three hours early. Go home, harbourmaster.

Talk to him through the door.

Hold the door. Wait him out.

Unbar the door and let him in.

The Board does not discuss arithmetic through a door, assessor.

Something heavy hits the door, and again, and on the third blow the rope parts with a crack like a shot and the door comes in on its hinges, and so does he.

He beats on the door for a quarter of an hour, steadily, like a man knocking in a fence post. The rope holds. On the still air the whole quay hears it, and counts the blows, and wonders.

Through the door, the sound of a tall wet man sitting down on the top step.

The Board has come up a great many stairs, assessor, to talk to a door. The Board will sit here a while. Then the Board will go home.

When the assessor unbars the door at last, he has gone. On the top step lies the thing he brought, laid square, and a puddle frozen in the shape of a man sitting down.

The assessor gives it to him in order: the time on the claim, the green in the rings, the drips the frost has kept from the weigh-house store to this room.

The Board has been counted. The Board had not expected to be counted tonight. The Board had expected to do the counting.

The Board does not recognise the Shelf's arithmetic, assessor. The Board has its own.

He comes up into the stair door with a boathook from the mole steps, twelve feet of ash and an iron hook, and has to bring it in sideways.

The Board's hammer was not in the Board's store, assessor. The Board inquired. The Board was told nothing, and drew its own conclusions, and a boathook.

In a glass room the boathook is a long, stupid thing. He cannot swing it without striking the panes.

The assessor takes the ash shaft in both hands and walks it backward out of his, hand over hand, like taking in a mooring line. He does not fight for it. He watches it go.

The shaft twists. The iron hook goes through a pane of the lamp-room glass behind the lens, and the frost blows in, and the harbourmaster lets go of the boathook as if it had bitten him and stands looking at the hole he has made in the wrong place.

The Board has broken a window. The Board is not, it appears, built for this.

He brings the iron of the hook round low, at the green rings, the only swing the room allows him.

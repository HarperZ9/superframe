A 4D or 5D shape can only be seen through a lens: a shadow, a slice or a diagram. Every view here says what its lens keeps and what it drops.

In plain words

plain

Picture a world as flat as a sheet of paper, with a square living in it. When a ball passes through that world, the square sees a dot that grows into a circle, shrinks back to a dot and vanishes. He never sees the ball. He sees one slice of it at a time.

We stand where the square stands, one step up. A tesseract passing through our space would show up as a solid that appears, grows, changes and disappears. The slider in the toy moves that cutting plane. With the tesseract held straight, every slice is a cube of the same size; tilt it with the xw, yw or zw knobs and the slices turn into other solids that swell and shrink as the plane sweeps.

Shadows are the other way in. A cube's shadow on a wall is flat: a square inside a square with the corners joined. The tesseract's shadow in our space is a cube inside a cube. A shadow keeps some facts and drops others. The plain shadow keeps parallel lines but stacks points on top of each other. Perspective keeps straight lines but shrinks the far side. Stereographic projection bends edges into arcs and keeps every angle. The Schlegel view looks into one cell, so that nothing overlaps.

Turning works by planes. In 3D you think of turning about an axis, but the turn happens in a plane: xy, xz or yz. In 4D there are six such planes and in 5D ten, so the toy has a knob for each. The knobs that name w (or v) turn the shape through directions we cannot point to; that is when the inner and outer cubes trade places.

The strip under the toy shows the same family in each dimension. Drag a point and you get a segment; drag the segment sideways and you get a square; drag the square and you get a cube. Each step doubles the corners: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32.

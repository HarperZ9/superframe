Any band of any spectrum, and any field, can be shifted into sight, sound and touch, so long as each view says what it keeps and what it loses.

In plain words

plain

Your eyes catch one thin band of light, from violet at 380 nanometres to red at 750. Your ears catch sound from about 20 to 20 000 vibrations a second. Everything else goes by unnoticed: the heat glowing off your hand, the radio passing through the room, the cry of a hunting bat, the field around a wire, the way light twists as it travels.

The bridge works the same way every time. Pick a window on the hidden range. Stretch or squeeze it to fit a range you can sense. Then say what was lost on the way. A window ten times wider than the rainbow has to squeeze ten colours' worth of detail into one, so close neighbours become one colour. The page counts those losses in bits and names the parts of the wave it dropped.

Each bridge has a sight, a sound and a sentence, so the page works for people who cannot see it or cannot hear it. The figure caption says in words what the picture shows. The line under Play says in words what the sound is doing.

Whose ideas these are

  • Shifting a band you cannot sense into one you can: heterodyne bat detectors, which mix a bat's ultrasound with a steady tone (after Pierce and Griffin's 1938 recordings of bat ultrasound); Landsat false-colour images, which paint shortwave infrared as visible colour; audification, playing seismic records fast enough to hear (Speeth 1961, Hayward 1994).
  • Carrying one sense in another: Paul Bach-y-Rita's tactile vision substitution (1969), which let blind people feel a camera's picture on their skin; Peter Meijer's The vOICe (1992), which turns images into sound for blind users (@seeingwithsound).
  • One notation for sound and colour together: Channa Horwitz's Sonakinatography (1970), where one number grid sets time, colour and sound. Every view here reads one state and paints and plays it from that same state.
  • The physics: Planck (1900) and Wien (1893) for the glow of hot bodies; CIE 1931 colour matching (data CC BY-SA 4.0); the mel scale (Stevens, Volkmann and Newman 1937); Faraday's lines of force; Oersted's compass (1820); streamlines and line-integral convolution (Cabral and Leedom 1993); Stokes (1852), Poincare (1892) and Jones (1941) for polarisation; Fourier (1822) for heat; Gauss (1827) for curvature; Abbott's Flatland (1884) and Hinton's tesseract (1888) for higher dimensions.
  • The data: tree rings and Alpine climate from the ring-channel study; n and k from the materials library; the law signatures and the shapes from ndim.
